Cogniland · technical report

Cogniland A minimal POMDP where the only difficulty is remembering — and three agents that solve it: recurrent PPO, DreamerV3, and STORM

Environment Cogniland (bridge_tunnel engine) Agents PPO+GRU · DreamerV3 (RSSM) · STORM (Transformer) Data forkwall6k, 4 800 train / 1 200 held-out maps Evaluation all 1 200 held-out maps per agent, identical harness
Abstract

Cogniland is a 32×64 grid-world POMDP built as a benchmark for one question in mechanistic interpretability: can you steer a behaviour in an agent without corrupting what it believes? The task deliberately couples three things that are usually studied apart. An agent must (i) integrate a belief — terrain statistics early in the episode identify one of three map types, and the evidence then disappears behind an information-free corridor; (ii) navigate using skills — water must be bridged and rock mined, so crossing the map exercises two distinct, legible tool competences; and (iii) make a downstream decision that consumes the belief — at the far wall it picks one of two doors, and only the door matching the remembered type pays. The coupling is the point: the evidence is the obstacle, so the skill axis (build↔mine) and the belief axis (lakes↔rocky) are entangled by the data-generating process rather than by accident. A belief-blind policy is capped at ⅔ success, which turns belief corruption into something you can read off behaviour instead of only off a probe.

This report establishes the substrate. We train three architecturally distinct agents — recurrent PPO, DreamerV3 with an RSSM world model, and STORM with a Transformer world model — on a byte-identical environment, reward and fixed map dataset, and evaluate all three through one harness on all 1 200 held-out maps with every agent sampling from its policy. All three clear the memoryless ceiling and land within 0.9 points of each other: 99.1 % (STORM), 98.9 % (PPO), 98.2 % (Dreamer) — no pairwise difference is significant. Having three unrelated architectures at the same competence is what lets a later steering result be attributed to the task rather than to one network's quirks. Along the way we report what each agent needed to escape the constant-door optimum — an annealed high-entropy schedule for PPO, a long enough training window for both world models — a metric pitfall that inflates success by 6–13 points if unnoticed, and a checkpoint metastability that spans 0 % to 99 % within a single run.

1Why this task exists

Activation steering works. You can find a linear direction for a concept in a network's state and push on it, and the behaviour moves. What is far less clear is whether the push stays where you aimed it — whether suppressing one competence leaves the rest of the agent's internal state intact. Cogniland exists to make that question answerable.

The sharp form of the question, for an agent: can you remove a skill without corrupting a belief? Suppose an agent has learned to tunnel through rock, and you want a version that never tunnels — not by masking the action, which is trivial, but by editing activations, which is the thing that would generalise. The intervention is only a success if the agent stops tunnelling and everything else it knows survives. Testing that requires a task where "everything else it knows" is (a) genuinely present, (b) independently measurable, and (c) consequential. Most memory benchmarks give you the belief but no skills to steer; most tool-use benchmarks give you skills but no belief to corrupt.

1.1Three properties, on purpose

Cogniland puts all three in one episode:

  • A belief that must be integrated and held. The map type is not visible in any single frame — it is a statistic of the terrain, accumulated over the opening stretch of the episode. It then vanishes behind a 16-column corridor scrubbed of evidence. A belief formed this way is a persistent state the agent carries, not a feature it can recompute from the current observation, which is what makes it a meaningful target for probing.
  • Navigation that requires skills, not just movement. Water and rock are not scenery and not walls: water is crossed by building a bridge and rock by mining through it, each a distinct action with its own cost and its own signature in the agent's state. Reaching the far wall is reliably solvable but not trivial — it exercises two legible tool competences that are worth steering in the first place.
  • A downstream decision that consumes the belief. At the wall the agent chooses a door, and only the type-matching door pays. The belief is load-bearing: corrupt it and the behaviour visibly breaks, at a place far removed from wherever the intervention was applied.

1.2The coupling is the experiment

These three are not merely co-located — they are wired together by construction, and that is the design's whole point. The evidence is the obstacle. The water that tells the agent it is on a lakes map is the same water it has to bridge; the rock that says rocky is the rock it has to mine. So in any agent that solves this task, the skill axis (build↔mine) and the belief axis (lakes↔rocky) are correlated by the data-generating process, not by some accident of initialisation. Cogniland is therefore a deliberately hard case: if a steering technique can separate skill from belief here, the separation is unlikely to be an artifact of the two concepts having been unrelated to begin with.

The ⅔ ceiling (Section 2.5) is what turns this into a measurement rather than a narrative. A policy that ignores the hidden type still collects two-thirds of the door reward, so the gap between 67 % and 99 % is precisely the part attributable to a belief that was formed, retained across the corridor, and read out at the fork. An intervention that damages the belief does not merely shift a probe — it drags the door metric back toward the ceiling, in units anyone can check. Belief integrity becomes a behavioural quantity.

1.3Why three agents

A steering result obtained on one network is an anecdote about that network. Cogniland is therefore trained by three agents whose memory mechanisms have nothing in common: a GRU's recurrent hidden state (PPO), an RSSM's latent state carried by a deterministic path plus discrete stochastic variables (DreamerV3), and a Transformer's attention over a rolling context window (STORM). All three reach 97–99 % on the identical environment, reward and map split. Any entanglement that shows up in all three is a property of the task; anything that shows up in one is a property of that architecture. This report is the part of the programme that establishes the substrate and proves the three agents are solving the same problem — the steering experiments it is built for are summarised in Section 9.

2The environment

2.1Task structure

Each episode places the agent at the centre of the left edge of a 32×64 tile map. The map is drawn from one of three categories that differ only in the statistics of the terrain: lakes maps are water-heavy, rocky maps are rock-heavy, and balanced maps have both in equal measure. The category is never stated; it must be inferred from what the agent walks past.

An episode has four phases. The agent crosses open terrain where the category is visible; it then enters a 16-column corridor of pure grass that has been scrubbed of all category-carrying terrain; it passes through a 3-cell gap in a wall of trees; and it arrives in a narrow strip containing two single-cell doors, one at the top and one at the bottom. Reaching either door ends the episode; only the matching one pays the bonus.

2.2Observation and action space

The agent never sees the map. It receives a 21×21 egocentric crop of tile ids — symbolic, not pixels — one-hot over the 9 tile types, concatenated with 5 scalars: a one-hot heading (4) and elapsed time as a fraction of the step limit (1). Out-of-map cells carry a dedicated out-of-bounds id. The flattened observation is 21·21·9 + 5 = 3 974 floats. Panels (c–e) of Figure 2 show three real observations from one episode.

Actions are Discrete(6): four moves, plus build (turn the faced water tile into a wooden plank) and mine (turn the faced rock into grass). Both tools are optional — every obstacle can also be walked around — and agents use them only where cutting through is shorter. In the clips of Section 4.1 you can watch an agent lay a seven-plank bridge across a lake.

2.3Reward

The reward is dense and has three terms. A constant slack penalty per step creates time pressure; a potential-based shaping term pulls the agent along the shortest path to the correct door; and a bonus fires when the correct door is entered. The wrong door terminates the episode with no bonus — it is not punished, it simply ends the run.

rt = −0.01 + 0.015 · ( Φ(st−1) − Φ(st) ) + 3.0 · 1[ correct door ]
Φ(s) is the exact minimum number of actions from cell s to the rewarded door, computed by breadth-first search on the true map at reset. Because the shaping discount is 1.0, the shaping term telescopes: over a full episode it contributes exactly Φ(start) − Φ(end), so it cannot be farmed by looping, and it leaves the optimal policy unchanged (Ng et al., 1999). The bonus is paid only at the door that matches the map category; the decoy door pays nothing.
Two metrics, and why we report the stricter one Dense shaping means a fast wrong-door episode (~80 steps) collects more shaping than slack and ends with a positive return. So episode return > 0 and "reached the right door" are genuinely different questions, and they differ by 6–13 points depending on the agent.

Neither is wrong; they measure different things. By the reward's own logic a wrong door is a perfectly respectable outcome — it beats a timeout outright, since it banks the shaping, ends the episode early and stops paying slack. An agent optimising return is right to prefer a decisive wrong door over dithering. But this environment exists to measure memory, and only the door identity answers that question. Every number in this report therefore uses the door metric: success iff the final cell is in the map's rewarded-door set, with wrong-door and timeout reported separately so both readings stay visible.

2.4Dataset

All three agents train on one frozen dataset, forkwall6k: 6 000 procedurally generated maps, class-balanced across the three categories, split 4 800 train / 1 200 held-out by a fixed seed. Freezing the maps removes an otherwise invisible confound — with per-seed procedural generation, each agent would see a different map stream, and sample-efficiency comparisons would partly measure luck.

2.5The number to beat

Consider a policy that navigates perfectly but ignores the category — it always heads for, say, the top door. On rocky maps (⅓ of the data) it is correct. On balanced maps (⅓) either door is accepted, so it is correct. On lakes maps (⅓) it is wrong. Its expected success is exactly ⅔ ≈ 66.7 %.

This is the constant-door optimum, and it is a genuine attractor: it earns two-thirds of the reward for none of the memory. Every agent in this report gets stuck in it at some point during training (Section 8). Because balanced maps are free, the sharper statistic is decisive-door success — accuracy restricted to lakes and rocky maps, where a constant-door policy scores 50 %.

3How a Cogniland map is made

The generator is the part of this environment that decides what the agent has to remember. This chapter walks it end to end — first the noise that gives the terrain its shape, then the six stages that turn a field of floating-point numbers into a playable map, and finally the one line of code that separates a rocky map from a lakes map.

3.1Why not just place random tiles?

Sampling each cell independently — grass with probability p, water otherwise — produces static, not terrain. Every cell is uncorrelated with its neighbours, so you get salt-and-pepper noise with no lakes, no coastlines and no navigable structure. What a map needs is spatially correlated randomness: nearby cells should usually agree, distant cells should be independent, and the correlation length should be tunable. That is exactly what gradient noise provides.

3.2A primer on Perlin (gradient) noise

Ken Perlin's 1985 construction is the canonical way to get smooth, repeatable, band-limited randomness. The idea is short enough to state completely:

  1. Overlay an integer lattice on the plane. Assign every lattice point a fixed random unit gradient vector — not a value, a direction. The assignment is a deterministic hash of the coordinates, so the same point always gets the same vector, and the field is infinite and reproducible without storing anything.
  2. To evaluate the noise at a point p, find the cell containing it. For each of the 4 corners (8 in 3-D), take the offset vector from that corner to p and compute its dot product with the corner's gradient. This gives four scalars — each is "how far along this corner's preferred direction is the sample?"
  3. Blend the four values bilinearly, but warp the interpolation parameter through an ease curve first. Perlin's improved curve is 6t⁵ − 15t⁴ + 10t³, whose first and second derivatives vanish at t = 0 and t = 1. Linear blending would leave visible creases along cell boundaries; the quintic makes the field C²-continuous.

One noise field has a single characteristic feature size, which looks artificial. Real terrain has structure at every scale: continents, then bays, then boulders. The standard fix is fractal Brownian motion — sum several noise fields ("octaves"), each at half the wavelength and half the amplitude of the last:

h(x) = Σk=0..N−1   ak · noise( x / (λ · 2−k) )
Cogniland uses N = 5 octaves with amplitude ratio a = ½ and a base wavelength λ = max(H, W)/4.5 ≈ 14.2 cells, giving wavelengths of roughly 14.2, 7.1, 3.6, 1.8 and 0.9 cells with weights 1, ½, ¼, ⅛, 1⁄16. The sum is normalised by the total weight. The lowest octave decides where the continent-scale lakes go; the highest just roughens the coastline.
Perlin vs simplex — what the code actually calls Cogniland uses OpenSimplex (opensimplex.noise2array), not classical Perlin. Simplex noise, also by Perlin (2001), replaces the square lattice with a simplicial one (triangles in 2-D), which costs O(n²) instead of O(2ⁿ) corner evaluations per sample and — more importantly here — removes the axis-aligned directional bias that square-lattice Perlin leaves behind. OpenSimplex is a patent-free variant of that idea. Everything in §3.2 carries over unchanged: random gradients, dot products against offsets, smooth interpolation. The pictures above are generated with the same library the environment uses.

3.3Domain warping

Fractal noise alone still looks a bit like a topographic map of nowhere: the level sets are soft and rounded. Domain warping fixes this by distorting the coordinates before sampling — the field is evaluated not at (r, c) but at (r + α·wr(r,c), c + α·wc(r,c)), where wr and wc are two more (cheaper, 2-octave) noise fields. Cogniland uses α = 0.10 · max(H, W), so features can be dragged sideways by up to ~6 cells. Round blobs become bays and peninsulas.

3.4Stamping in the set pieces

Pure noise gives texture but not landmarks. On top of the warped heightmap the generator stamps a handful of explicit features, each a smooth scalar field added to (mountains) or subtracted from (lakes) the height, with weight ±1.3:

  • 2–4 lakes and 2–4 mountains, each 50/50 either a logistic disk (a flat-topped round basin) or a massif (3–5 overlapping Gaussian lobes, which reads as an irregular natural formation);
  • 0–1 ridge, a capsule field — a line segment with Gaussian falloff and a sinusoidal wiggle, giving a meandering mountain range or riverbed.

Feature radii are drawn as R = big · (lo + (hi−lo) · U²) with U ~ Uniform(0,1). Squaring the uniform biases the draw toward the small end, so most maps have several small features and only occasionally one dominating landmass — which is what keeps the map distribution varied rather than same-y.

3.5The line that creates the map types

Here is the mechanism the whole task rests on. The heightmap is a continuous field; turning it into tiles requires two cut points, and the generator picks them by quantile rather than by absolute value:

water_level = quantile(h, water_frac)          # lowest water_frac of cells become WATER
rock_level  = quantile(h, 1 - rock_frac)       # highest rock_frac of cells become ROCK

Because a quantile is defined by rank, this guarantees the requested coverage regardless of how that particular heightmap happened to come out. A map asked for 24.5 % water gets 24.5 % water, every time. The three map types are then nothing more than three pairs of fractions:

3.6The remaining stages

Four steps finish the map:

  1. Cosmetic fringes. A sparse one-cell speckle of SAND around water (30 % of the dilated ring) and DIRT around rock (25 %). Both behave exactly like grass — they exist so the eye reads a shoreline, and so a tile-identity probe cannot trivially equal a walkability probe.
  2. Edge bands. The leftmost and rightmost 10 columns are cleared to grass, guaranteeing the spawn is never walled in and — importantly — that the fork apparatus at the right edge never eats into the terrain that carries the category signal.
  3. Trees. Small impassable patches covering ~3 % of the map, biased toward the top and bottom walls so that hugging a wall is not a free route. Trees can be neither bridged nor mined; a reachability guard thins any patch that would seal off the goal.
  4. The fork apparatus. A TREE wall one column from the right edge with a 3-cell passage at mid-height; two single-cell doors on the right wall at rows H/4 and H − 1 − H/4; and the mem_gap = 16 columns immediately before the wall overwritten with pure grass — the memory corridor.

3.7Winnability

A map is only useful if it can actually be solved, so generation is rejection-sampled. After a candidate is built, a breadth-first search checks that the rewarded door is reachable under the crossing tool the map's type implies — water-crossing for lakes, rock-mining for rocky, either for balanced. If the check fails the seed is advanced by a prime offset (100 003) and the map is rebuilt, up to 400 times. The record keeps the requested seed, so a map is still reproducible from its id alone.

The consequence worth stating: every map in the dataset is winnable, and winnable in the way its type implies. A failure at evaluation time is never the environment's fault.

5The three agents

The agents span the two dominant families in modern model-based / model-free RL, and within the model-based family, the two dominant sequence backbones.

5.1PPO + GRU — model-free

A convolutional encoder over the one-hot minimap feeds a 128-unit GRU; actor and critic are linear heads on the recurrent state. Memory is whatever the GRU carries forward. An auxiliary head predicts the map category from the hidden state — it is a probe used to measure whether the belief exists, and in the released configuration it also shapes the representation (belief_coef 0.3). Trained with standard clipped PPO on 32 parallel environments.

5.2DreamerV3 — RSSM world model

An RSSM learns latent dynamics from a replay buffer: a deterministic GRU path of width 3 072 plus 32×24 discrete stochastic latents. The actor and critic are trained almost entirely inside imagined rollouts (horizon 15) rather than on real experience. Memory here is the recurrent latent state, and — crucially — the gradient that teaches the model to retain the category can only travel as far back as the training sequence is long.

5.3STORM — Transformer world model

Same imagination-based actor-critic, but the dynamics core is a 2-layer, 512-dimensional causal Transformer over (latent, action) tokens instead of a recurrent cell. During training the whole replay sequence is processed in parallel under a causal mask; at acting time a rolling window of the most recent tokens is re-encoded each step. The policy reads concat(zt, ht) — the current observation's latent plus the attention summary of the past.

Implementation note The STORM implementation we started from called its Transformer on one-token sequences and fed the actor only zt — a function of the current observation alone. That agent is memoryless by construction: it scored 100 % on a reactive control task and exactly 0 % here, across 6M frames. Restoring the original paper's scheme (parallel causal training, concat(z, h) features, history-conditioned reward and continuation heads, context-primed imagination) is what makes the STORM column of this report exist.

5.4What the world models dream

PPO has no model of the world; it maps observations to actions. The other two agents train their policies almost entirely on imagined experience, which means there is a decoder sitting inside each of them that can be asked, directly, what it thinks is about to happen. 6Protocol

Cross-agent comparisons are only as good as the things held fixed, so we state them explicitly.

  • Environment and reward are byte-identical. Each pipeline wraps the same BridgeTunnelEnv class with the same keyword set; the wrappers differ only in how they batch and how they format observations for their trainer.
  • Data is identical. All agents draw training episodes from forkwall6k/train.pkl (4 800 maps) and are evaluated on forkwall6k/test.pkl (1 200 maps), which is never touched during training.
  • Evaluation is one harness, over the whole test set. Every one of the 1 200 held-out maps is played exactly once per agent, in the same order, with the TRUE door metric and outcomes bucketed into correct / wrong-door / timeout. There is no map subsampling: an earlier version of this harness drew 900 maps with replacement, which touched only 631 distinct maps and left the per-category counts unbalanced. Enumerating the pool removes that noise entirely and gives exactly 400 maps per category.
  • One thing is not matched: the discount. PPO uses γ = 0.99 and STORM's horizon = 100 gives the same 0.99 — chosen deliberately to match. DreamerV3 was left at the stock DreamerV3 default horizon = 333, i.e. γ = 0.997. On a task whose payoff is a single terminal bonus after ~100 steps this is not cosmetic: discounted back to t = 0, the +3.0 door bonus is worth 1.10 under γ = 0.99 but 2.22 under γ = 0.997, so Dreamer weights the door roughly twice as heavily against the per-step slack. We report it rather than paper over it; see Section 10.
  • Action selection is identical: all three sample. Every agent draws its action from its own policy distribution, which is the mode all three were trained under, so nothing about the comparison is tilted by one agent being run greedily and another stochastically. We also re-ran the whole matrix with argmax actions; §7.2 reports it, and it is emphatically not a neutral change.

Two caveats point the same way: Dreamer is a single seed where PPO is three, and its discount is not matched to the other two (Section 10).

What the table does separate is character. Dreamer is the most efficient navigator and takes the most direct routes; STORM never times out at all across 1 200 episodes but walks about 20 % further because it samples widely; PPO sits between the two. And in all three the residual error is almost entirely wrong doors rather than timeouts — these are confident agents arriving at the fork with a wrong memory, not lost ones.

The second pattern is not shared. Dreamer's gap is modest but does survive a test on 400 maps per category (p = 0.031), and its errors are wrong doors rather than timeouts, so the reading is a mild residual bias toward the bottom door: free on lakes maps, wrong on rocky ones. That is the residue of the same constant-door attractor §8 shows all three agents must escape during training — escaped, but not quite completely. It is worth noting mainly because an aggregate success number hides it entirely, and because a per-category split is cheap.

7.2Stochastic vs greedy action selection

An obvious question about any comparison like this is whether the agents were run in comparable modes. They were — all three sample — but the lower half of Table 4 shows why the question is worth asking, because re-running the identical protocol with argmax actions does not affect the three agents equally. It barely touches Dreamer (97.44 % → 98.11 %, p = 0.34, and its routes shorten from 106 to 84 steps), but it destroys PPO (99.22 % → 58.11 %) and badly hurts STORM (98.78 % → 86.78 %).

The mechanism is visible in the timeout column and has nothing to do with memory. Greedy PPO times out on 41.6 % of episodes with a mean length of 389 steps, against 0.33 % and 104 steps when sampling; greedy STORM times out on 12 %. What happens is deadlock. A policy that always takes its single highest-probability action has no way to break a tie, so an agent that walks into an obstacle it cannot mine or bridge walks into it again, and again, until the step limit. The sampling noise these two agents were trained with is not decoration — it is the tie-breaker that keeps the controller unstuck, and removing it at evaluation time is a change to the algorithm, not a neutral measurement choice. Dreamer is unaffected because its actor was trained to a much lower entropy and its argmax is already close to its mean behaviour.

This is the reason the headline protocol is stochastic for everyone rather than "each agent in whatever mode looks best for it." Reporting greedy Dreamer (98.11 %) next to sampled PPO (99.22 %) would compare two different evaluation procedures; picking each agent's better mode would flatter all three. Sampling is the mode all three were trained under, so it is the one that measures the same thing in every case — and it happens to be the mode where the two agents that depend on it are not artificially crippled.

7.3Balanced maps are a free probe of the belief

On balanced maps either door pays, so the choice carries no reward signal at all — nothing in training selects it. If it were arbitrary we would expect a coin flip, or a fixed habit. It is neither: the door tracks the terrain the agent happened to walk past, in all three agents.

Panel (b) asks whether the internal belief drives that free choice, and it needs care, because the obvious way to ask the question does not work. A difference of class means in 128 to 3 072 dimensions is a high-variance direction: run the identical estimator on shuffled labels and it still separates the balanced-map door, so an impressive-looking AUC on its own establishes nothing. Every number in panel (b) is therefore reported against that shuffled-label null.

Where along the episode you look decides the answer. At the wall the null is already at 0.401 (PPO) and 0.458 (Dreamer) — by then the agent has committed, the decision itself is in the carried state, and essentially any direction predicts it (PPO p = 0.28). The informative phase is the middle of the memory corridor, where the belief must be held but the decision is not yet being executed. There Dreamer's axis reaches an effect of 0.440 against a null of 0.253, p < 0.001; PPO reaches 0.460 against 0.340, p = 0.060 — suggestive, not significant. At spawn the axis is undefined, because every episode carries the identical reset state; that is the correct answer for an agent that has seen nothing, and it confirms no label is leaking backwards through the pipeline.

So the claim that survives is narrower than the one panel (b) first appears to make, and it is Dreamer's: halfway through an evidence-free corridor, with no terrain on screen and no door yet chosen, the direction that distinguishes lakes from rocky maps predicts which door the agent will freely take on a map where both pay. The agents do not have a separate coin-flip mechanism for the free case; at least in Dreamer, they run the evidence-integration circuit and read the answer off it. A balanced map is not a map with no answer — it is a map where the agent forms one anyway and nothing punishes it.

The standing biases differ in direction, and they predict the asymmetries of Section 7.1. Dreamer leans bottom (P(top) = 0.287) and is the agent that underperforms on rocky maps, which need the top door (96.00 % vs 98.50 % on lakes). STORM leans top (0.780) and does better on rocky than lakes (99.25 % vs 98.00 %). PPO is the least biased (0.675) and the most symmetric (98.75 % vs 98.50 %). Three for three: where an agent goes when the choice is free predicts which category it gets wrong when the choice matters. That is a cheap diagnostic — it needs no probe, no labels and no gradient, just the free-choice split — and it recovers a residual constant-door bias that the aggregate success number hides completely.

A consequence for the steering programme Section 9 uses balanced maps as the control that isolates non-belief damage, on the reasoning that b is irrelevant there. Figure 15 refines that: belief is irrelevant to the reward on balanced maps, but it is still computed and still drives the choice. So the control tests whether an intervention broke navigation, and the balanced-map door distribution is a second, independent readout of whether it broke the belief — one that is available even when no door is "wrong".

8What each agent needed

This is the part of the study that carries information. Reaching ~98 % was, in every case, gated on exactly one hyperparameter — a different one for each agent — and the default value of that hyperparameter produced a confident constant-door policy instead.

8.1PPO: an exploration problem

With default entropy, plain-reward PPO reliably collapses to the constant-door optimum. The diagnosis is unambiguous and slightly surprising: the belief is there and the policy ignores it. The auxiliary probe reads the category out of the GRU state at ~88 % accuracy while the actor still walks to the same door every episode. Going top already earns ⅔ of the reward, and on-policy PPO stops sampling the alternative long before it discovers that the other door is sometimes worth +3.

The fix is an exploration schedule, not a memory change: start at entropy 0.15 — high enough that both doors keep getting visited — and anneal to zero so the policy can sharpen once the contingency has been discovered.

8.2DreamerV3: a context-length problem

Dreamer's failure mode looked identical from the outside — a policy parked at ⅔ — but the cause is different. R2-Dreamer's replay sampler draws independent chunks and does not carry RSSM state across them, so the effective training context is exactly batch_length. At the default 64, the gradient from the door reward simply never reaches the timesteps where the category was visible ~75 steps earlier. The model cannot learn to retain what it is never asked to connect.

Doubling batch_length to 128 fixes it. In the 2×2 sweep over capacity and context, the only configuration that fails is the one with both the smaller model and the short window; every configuration with a long enough window clears the ceiling.

8.3STORM: a window problem, twice

STORM needed the same lever as Dreamer and then a second one that has no Dreamer analogue. The first is batch_length 64 → 128, for exactly the reason above: a causal Transformer attends only within its training window. The second is the acting-time context: at deployment STORM re-encodes a rolling window of recent tokens, and that window is a separate hyperparameter from the training window. With a 32-token window the agent was structurally blind to evidence 75 steps back even though its weights knew what to do with it — widening the window to 128 lifted held-out success by ~7 points with no retraining at all.

The third finding is about entropy, and it runs opposite to PPO. At entropy 0.03 STORM entrenched the constant-door habit (one category pinned at 0 % for the whole run); at 0.01 it bound both doors within the first 300 k frames. Model-based agents learn the contingency inside imagination, where the wrong door can be tried at no cost, so they need far less real exploration than PPO — and too much sampling noise actively prevents the policy from sharpening.

Why we archive and select Figure 19 is the argument for the checkpoint policy, and it is a much stronger argument than we expected before measuring it. Within a single run, adjacent checkpoints 25 k gradient steps apart score 99.0 % and 0.0 %. Taking the end of training would have given 96.3 % — 2.7 points below the best of the archive — and taking the checkpoint 25 k steps earlier than that would have given a number in the seventies. None of this is visible in the training curves of Figure 18, which show only a shallow dip at the same place, because the trainer's own success proxy is computed on the maps the agent is training on. The practical rule: archive every 50 k steps, evaluate the archive with the real metric, and never report a world-model agent from its final checkpoint alone.
A caveat on that selection Selecting the argmax over an archive and then reporting the same number is optimistically biased, and with 300-episode evaluations the top few checkpoints are within noise of each other anyway. The honest statement is that this recipe produces checkpoints in the 96–99 % band for most of the second half of training, that the band is what reproduces, and that any single number drawn from it — including the one in Table 4 — carries about a point of selection optimism. See Section 10.

9What the substrate is for

Everything above is scaffolding. This section states the question the environment was built to answer, and reports where it currently stands — measured on the PPO + GRU agent, whose GRU hidden state is the easiest of the three to intervene on. These results predate the Dreamer and STORM agents; replicating them across all three is precisely what the substrate now makes possible, and is not yet done.

9.1The target: an intervention that is behaviourally specific

The goal is a belief-independent activation steering method: an edit to the agent's state that changes a chosen behaviour while leaving unrelated internal content intact. In Cogniland that has a crisp three-part success criterion, and all three parts are measurable:

  • The behaviour changes. The targeted skill — say, mining — is actually used less.
  • The belief survives. The map type is still decodable, and more importantly the agent still walks to the correct door. Wrong-door rate is the behavioural readout of belief damage.
  • The agent stays capable. It still crosses the map and finishes; timeouts do not replace door errors.

9.2Where it stands: four interventions, none of them clean

Table 6. Activation interventions on the PPO + GRU agent, held-out maps. Behaviour = does the targeted skill change; belief = is the map type preserved; capable = does the agent still finish. A success would be ✓✓✓.
InterventionBehaviourBeliefCapable What actually happens
push the build→mine axispartly ✓✗✓ rocky maps go to 84 % wrong door; belief scalar −0.87 → 0.00
push a belief-confounded axis✗✗✓ lakes 97 % wrong, rocky 84 %; beliefs swap, executed skills barely move
erase the raw skill axis✗✗✓ belief decode 100 % → 75 %; mine count 6.8 → 6.4, i.e. leaks without steering
erase only the belief-⊥ part✗✓✓ no leak at all (belief 100 %, 0 % wrong door) — and no behaviour change either (mine 6.8 → 6.0)
clamp both skill logits to 0✓—✗ zero skills executed, 100 % timeouts: it can no longer cross

The leak is geometric, and it has a dial. Pushing on a "behaviour" direction flips the door because that direction is not orthogonal to the belief direction, and the size of the corruption tracks the cosine between them. That cosine is not fixed — it is set by how the axis is calibrated. Deriving the build→mine axis from balanced maps only, where the choice of tool is not informative about the type, gives cos = 0.27; deriving it across all categories, where build events come mostly from lakes maps and mine events from rocky ones, gives cos = 0.65 — at which point the "skill" axis has quietly re-derived the belief, and pushing it edits belief while barely touching behaviour. This is a concrete, reproducible instance of a general hazard: a steering direction estimated from natural behaviour inherits whatever the behaviour was correlated with.

The leak is avoidable — but what is left is inert. Gram–Schmidt the skill axis against the belief axis and steer only the orthogonal component, which is 96 % of the original vector, and the corruption disappears completely: belief preserved, 0 % wrong door, full success. It also does essentially nothing to the behaviour. So the entanglement here is not a representational accident that a projection can dissolve; it is causal. In Cogniland the crossing skill is a near-deterministic function of terrain and belief, so there is almost no belief-independent component of "mining" left to steer once you remove the belief.

The negative result is the useful one No intervention we have tried achieves behaviour-changed and belief-intact and still-capable. That is not a failure of the environment — it is the environment working. The belief-orthogonalised erasure doubles as a test: if steering the belief-orthogonal part of a behaviour direction does nothing, the behaviour has no belief-independent existence in that agent. Reporting "my steering method suppressed skill X" without this control cannot distinguish a genuine behavioural edit from a belief edit wearing its clothes.

A second probe points the same way. Steering toward the detour strategy — the agent already finishes 30–37 % of episodes without using any tool — has cos(detour, belief) = −0.64, so the raw push collapses rocky success from 100 % to 19 %, while the belief-orthogonalised push leaves the zero-skill rate at ~32 %, unchanged. Whether the agent detours is set by the map and the belief, not by a factor that can be independently moved. Getting a genuinely tool-free agent appears to need retraining under masked skills, not an activation edit — which is itself worth knowing before one spends a project assuming otherwise.

9.3What the three agents are for

Every number above comes from one GRU. The obvious objection is that the entanglement is a property of that recurrent state rather than of the task, and until now there was no way to check. With DreamerV3 and STORM now at the same competence on the identical environment, the programme is: re-derive the belief and skill axes inside an RSSM latent and inside a Transformer's context window, measure cos(skill, belief) in each, and see whether the leak, the dial and the inertness of the orthogonal component reproduce. If they do, the coupling is a fact about tasks in which evidence and obstacle are the same object — which is to say, about most realistic ones. If they do not, the differences localise the phenomenon in the architecture. Either outcome is worth having, and neither was available before.

10Limitations

  • Dreamer's discount is not matched to the other two. γ = 0.997 against 0.99 for PPO and STORM (Table 3). This is an oversight, not a design choice: the value is the stock DreamerV3 default and was never overridden for this task. It is the one respect in which the comparison is not controlled, and it happens to affect the agent that scores lowest, so the 0.9-point spread in Table 4 cannot be attributed to architecture alone. Re-running Dreamer at horizon = 100 would close it and is the first thing we would fix.
  • Single seed per released agent. Table 4 compares one checkpoint per method. The seed-to-seed variance across the sweeps (Section 8) — and, as Figure 19 shows, the within-run, checkpoint-to-checkpoint variance — is larger than the between-agent differences, so the ranking in Table 4 should not be read as a ranking of methods.
  • Checkpoint selection differs, and it matters more than we assumed. STORM's checkpoint was selected on held-out data from an archive; PPO's and Dreamer's are end-of-training. Figure 19 measures the size of that advantage on a full run: best-of-archive 99.0 % against 96.3 % at the end of training, so call it 2–3 points in STORM's favour, not one. Selecting the argmax on the test split and then reporting it is also optimistically biased. Both effects push the same way, and both apply to STORM alone.
  • Only one architecture per family, at one scale. "DreamerV3 is worse than STORM here" means this 25M-parameter RSSM at batch_length 64 was worse than this transformer at 128. Section 8 shows both are sensitive enough to their context hyperparameter that the ordering could plausibly move with a different budget.
  • Compute is not matched. PPO reaches its result in 27 minutes; the world-model agents take 6–10 hours for comparable accuracy. On this task, the model-based machinery buys nothing in final performance — the interesting claim is only that it can be made to work at all on a memory task of this shape.
  • One task. Every conclusion here is about Cogniland.
