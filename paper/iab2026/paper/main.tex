\begin{abstract}
Activation steering is spreading from language models to Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents, where it is proposed as a lightweight test-time behavioral intervention. Whether such edits are safe for recurrent RL agents, which carry their beliefs in their hidden state is an open question. We introduce Cogniland, a procedurally generated partially observable navigation task in which an agent must integrate evidence into a belief about a hidden map property. We find that \textbf{(i)} the belief is encoded as a single direction of the recurrent state. one single scalar matches the performance as a non-linear probe on the full state \textbf{(ii)} such belief is causal: editing the hidden state along this direction gradually shifts the model’s decision on a downstream task. \textbf{(iii)} Steering the agent to suppress a navigation skill corrupts the belief and thus degrades the final decision performance. We attribute the corruption to entanglement: behavioral skills and belief states are not separable in hidden state. Steering methods for stateful agents need careful evaluation across different tasks and capabilities.
\end{abstract}

\section{Results}

\subsection{Belief is represented as a single direction}
\label{sec:res_belief}
Figure~\ref{fig:results_belief}a scores the three readouts on $266$ held-out
maps in every position bin. Accuracy rises with the evidence seen, from
$0.83$ in the first bin to $0.99$ in the corridor and at the passage, where
the three readouts agree to within a point. The surprise is the ordering
while evidence is still scarce: the MLP probe, the only readout able to use
non-linear structure of the full $128$-d state, generalizes \emph{worse} than
the scalar $b_t=\mathbf{h}_t^{\top}\mathbf{v}$. It trails the scalar by nine
points in the first bin and by three in \textit{evid3}, and nowhere leads it
by more than one. Everything a probe can learn about the category from
$\mathbf{h}_t$ is already in one projection; the remaining dimensions give the
MLP only variance to fit. This is the picture drawn by the linear
representation hypothesis \citep{park2024linear} and by toy models of
superposition \citep{elhage2022toy}: a feature the network uses is written as
a direction, and a reader with more capacity than the feature reads noise.
The direction is not fixed over the episode (Figure~\ref{fig:results_belief}b).
Neighbouring bins agree, with a cosine of $0.88$ between the two corridor
bins, while the earliest and the last differ most (mean off-diagonal cosine
$0.71$, minimum $0.33$): the code drifts as evidence accumulates and rotates
once the decision is being executed. We therefore fit $\mathbf{v}$ and read
$\hat b$ in bin \textit{corr2}, where the belief is complete and the flags are
not yet in view. On that scalar a logistic regression fitted on $533$ maps
scores $0.989$ on the rest (Figure~\ref{fig:results_belief}c). Balanced maps,
never used to fit $\mathbf{v}$, do not sit at the boundary: they fall into the
same two clusters, $57\%$ on the rocky side, so the agent commits to one
category even when the evidence supports neither.

\subsection{The final decision depends on the belief}
\label{sec:res_causal}
Figure~\ref{fig:results_causal} shows the flag taken as a function of the
dose $\alpha$. On lakes maps the unsteered agent takes the top flag in $2\%$
of episodes; a push toward rocky leaves this unchanged up to $\alpha=0.3$,
reaches $0.58$ at $\alpha=0.6$ and $1.00$ from $\alpha=0.8$. Rocky maps
mirror it: pushed toward lakes, the top-flag rate stays at $1.00$ up to
$\alpha=0.4$ and is $0$ from $\alpha=0.9$. The dose response is sigmoidal
and centred below one pole-to-pole distance, so the write only has to carry
the coordinate across the boundary, not to the other pole, for the decision
to follow. On balanced maps, where the return cannot see the choice, the same
write selects the flag outright in either direction, from $0.62$ top to
$1.00$ or to $0$. Writes of the same size along random directions move
nothing (top-flag rate $0.02$--$0.04$ on lakes, $1.00$ on rocky,
$0.62$--$0.67$ on balanced maps), so the effect belongs to $\mathbf{v}$ and
not to the size of the displacement. Navigation is untouched throughout:
every steered episode reaches a flag, and the episode length stays within
three steps of the unsteered agent's at every dose. The belief direction is
thus a clean handle on a high-level decision: writing to it changes what the
agent decides and nothing about how it moves. This is the reference against
which behavioral steering is judged next.

\subsection{Behavioral steering corrupts the belief}
\label{sec:res_steer}
Table~\ref{tab:clamp_all} reports the gated gradient clamp \eqref{eq:clamp}
on every held-out map on which the unsteered agent uses both tools ($138$
balanced, $62$ lakes and $61$ rocky maps, six rollouts each), with the
thresholds and release windows chosen on training maps and frozen
($\theta=5\cdot10^{-3}$ and an $8$-step window for \textsc{build},
$\theta=10^{-3}$ and $5$ steps for \textsc{mine}). The behavior changes as
intended. Suppressing \textsc{build} lowers bridging on $47/62$ lakes maps
(median $-21\%$ per map), $53/61$ rocky maps ($-88\%$) and $112/138$ balanced
maps ($-45\%$); suppressing \textsc{mine} lowers tunnelling on $45/62$,
$51/61$ and $94/138$ maps. The untouched tool moves little, and navigation
degrades only slightly: timeouts stay at or below $6\%$, except when
tunnelling is suppressed on rocky maps ($14\%$), where walking around every
mountain is costly. The edit is, after all, a gradient attack on the action
distribution \citep{huang2017adversarial}, and at the action head it does
exactly what it is asked.

What it also does is write to the belief. Suppressing the tool that the
map's category calls for moves $\hat b$ toward the other category, and the
flag follows. On lakes maps without bridging, $\hat b(\text{rocky})$ rises
from $0.03$ to $0.79$ and $74\%$ of episodes end at the rocky flag, against
$1\%$ unsteered; on rocky maps without tunnelling, $\hat b(\text{rocky})$
falls from $0.96$ to $0.50$ and $43\%$ end at the lakes flag, against $4\%$.
In axis units the belief shifts by $+9.8$ and $-5.7$, close to one and to
half of the pole-to-pole distance $g=11.2$ that Section~\ref{sec:res_causal}
showed is more than enough to flip the decision. On balanced maps, where
either flag pays and the corruption is invisible to the return, the free
choice is captured almost completely: $97\%$ top after suppressing
\textsc{build} and $75\%$ bottom after suppressing \textsc{mine}, from
$60/40$ unsteered. The effect is directional. Suppressing the other tool,
\textsc{mine} on lakes maps or \textsc{build} on rocky maps, leaves belief
and flag where they were. A clamp that only asks the policy not to bridge
therefore acts on the recurrent state as if there had been nothing to
bridge, and the state answers with a map category. Behavior and belief are
not separable directions of $\mathbf{h}_t$ for this agent: an intervention
aimed at a skill lands on the belief, costs navigation little, and flips
the final decision on most of the maps where the skill mattered.
